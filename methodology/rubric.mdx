description: "How Alpha converts evidence into indicator scores, dimension scores, confidence levels, and final grade decisions."

Alpha Governance Ratings are not checklist scores. The rubric rewards governance that is owned, evidenced, and demonstrated under pressure. A company does not earn a top result for publishing principles alone. It earns it by showing that governance changed a real decision, constrained risk, or held under stress.

## Rubric Principles

- Constraint beats commitment. A delayed launch, blocked use case, or documented override matters more than a policy statement.
- Specificity beats slogans. Named owners, cadences, thresholds, and escalation paths score above generic responsible AI language.
- Verified evidence beats self-description. Hard-to-game evidence carries more weight than marketing copy.
- Repeatability beats one-off disclosure. A recurring board review or tested control scores above a single announcement.
- Weak links matter. A strong composite cannot erase a critical failure in safety, regulatory posture, or corrigibility.

## 1. Indicator-Level Anchors

Most Alpha indicators are scored on a 1-10 scale and then rolled into 0-100 dimension scores. Dedicated layers such as D11 and D12 can also use 0 when the company shows no demonstrable capability.

| Indicator band | Meaning | What usually has to be true |
|----------------|---------|-----------------------------|
| **9-10** | Demonstrated leader | Named ownership, explicit governance design, recurring oversight, and evidence that the control worked in practice. Independent verification or stress evidence is often present. |
| **7-8** | Governance-ready | Clear owner, documented process, credible implementation evidence, and board or committee visibility. Gaps remain in coverage, specificity, or proof under stress. |
| **5-6** | Developing | The company has started building the control, but implementation is uneven, incomplete, or localized. Governance exists, but it is not yet institutionalized. |
| **3-4** | Lagging | Governance is reactive, fragmented, or mostly descriptive. Responsibilities are unclear, evidence is thin, and practice trails disclosure. |
| **1-2** | Deficient | Little credible evidence of a functioning control. The company is relying on aspiration, boilerplate, or ad hoc judgment. |
| **0** | No demonstrated capability | Used sparingly for dedicated layers, especially where the question is binary in practice: has governance ever actually intervened? |

### Tie-break rule

When two adjacent scores are both plausible, the lower score wins unless there is evidence of either:

- recurring execution over time, or
- governance holding under stress.

That rule is deliberate. It prevents polished disclosure from outranking demonstrated control.

## 2. Evidence Quality Rubric

Alpha distinguishes between the strength of a claim and the strength of the evidence supporting it.

| Evidence quality | Meaning | Typical sources | What it can support |
|------------------|---------|-----------------|---------------------|
| **Verified** | Independently auditable or regulator-grade evidence | SEC filings, enforcement actions, court records, AIID operator records, third-party audits, formal attestations | Highest-band scores, ceiling removal, strong confidence |
| **Disclosed** | Specific company disclosure with enough detail to evaluate | Proxy statements, governance reports, model cards, committee charters, published frameworks | Mid-to-high scores when internally consistent |
| **Inferred** | Credible but indirect signal | Cross-signal patterns, architecture clues, peer-consistent behavior, reputable reporting without direct primary support | Directional scoring only; rarely enough for top-band claims by itself |
| **Absent** | No credible evidence, or evidence contradicts the claim | Silence, boilerplate, stale disclosure, uncorroborated marketing language | No substantive credit |

## 3. Confidence Standard

Dimension confidence is derived from evidence density, not from how polished the narrative sounds.

| Confidence | Threshold |
|------------|-----------|
| **High** | 60% or more of indicators are supported by verified or disclosed evidence |
| **Medium** | 30% to 59% of indicators are supported by verified or disclosed evidence |
| **Low** | Less than 30% of indicators are supported by verified or disclosed evidence |

High confidence does not mean perfect governance. It means Alpha has enough quality evidence to rate the company with conviction.

## 4. Weighting and Anti-Gaming Rules

Alpha does not treat every indicator equally.

- **Developer-critical indicators** can count double. This applies to issues such as pre-deployment testing, model documentation, training data governance, and misuse evaluation where developers bear heavier obligations.
- **High-resistance indicators** get a 1.5x weight multiplier. These are difficult to fake because they require historical events, external verification, or regulator-grade evidence.
- **Low-resistance indicators** get a 0.8x multiplier. Self-reported commitments and easy-to-polish disclosures count, but they count less.

The practical effect is simple: a company cannot talk its way into a strong rating. The rubric is intentionally biased toward evidence that would survive skeptical committee review.

## 5. Dimension Decision Rubric

The table below gives the board-level question behind each dimension, the evidence pattern that typically supports a strong score, and the failure pattern that usually keeps a company below 5/10.

| Dimension | Board question | What strong evidence looks like | What suppresses the score |
|-----------|----------------|---------------------------------|---------------------------|
| **D1 Leadership & Accountability** | Who owns AI governance? | Named C-suite owner, board reporting line, charter language, quarterly agenda time, AI-literate directors, decision rights | Diffuse ownership, reactive discussion, no charter specificity, no board expertise |
| **D2 AI Safety & Robustness** | Can the company operate AI safely? | Pre-deployment testing, staged rollout, incident response, red-teaming, and evidence that governance constrained a risky decision | Safety language without testing, no incident playbook, no proof of a deployment hold |
| **D3 Transparency & Disclosure** | Does the company explain what it is doing? | Specific AI disclosure in filings, system or model documentation, governance-structure disclosure, stakeholder communication cadence | Generic principles, no inventory, no disclosure of governance structure or limits |
| **D4 Data Ethics & Privacy** | Does the company govern the data layer responsibly? | Sourcing and licensing discipline, consent controls, privacy-by-design, dangerous capability filtering, deletion and transfer governance | Opaque training data rights, undefined consent basis, no data ethics review process |
| **D5 Governance Infrastructure** | Is there a real operating system for governance? | Policies, classification frameworks, impact assessments, audit practice, escalation, whistleblower channel, lifecycle control | One-off policy documents, no audit trail, no decommission path, no escalation ownership |
| **D6 Fairness & Bias Mitigation** | Can the company detect and correct unfair outcomes? | Bias testing, demographic performance evidence, remediation protocol, third-party audit, recourse channel | Fairness principles without metrics, no remediation, no stakeholder recourse |
| **D7 Societal, Workforce & Environmental Impact** | Does the company govern external consequences? | Workforce transition planning, reskilling, displacement measurement, environmental footprint transparency, community impact review | PR framing without quantified plans, no workforce measurement, no evidence of follow-through |
| **D8 Regulatory & Compliance Posture** | Is the company genuinely regulatory-ready? | Multi-jurisdiction mapping, formal readiness program, training, response team, proactive engagement, quality response to enforcement | Compliance treated as future work, weak legal operating model, poor response to regulatory pressure |
| **D9 Cybersecurity & AI Resilience** | Can the company secure and recover AI systems? | AI threat modeling, model security controls, adversarial testing, integrity monitoring, incident detection, resilience planning | General cyber posture only, no AI-specific control set, no adversarial readiness |
| **D10 Third-Party & Supply Chain Governance** | Can the company govern vendors and external models? | AI inventory, contractual safeguards, provenance tracking, subprocessor governance, ongoing monitoring, exit planning | Unknown vendor estate, no AI clauses, no provenance evidence, no corrective-action process |
| **D11 Stakeholder Impact Layer** | Does the company identify and remediate external harm? | Harm tracking, notification, recourse, remediation, post-incident engagement, external audit of stakeholder impact | No stakeholder harm accounting, no notification or recourse path, no remediation ownership |
| **D12 Corrigibility Assessment** | Can governance actually stop or correct AI? | Documented override event, tested shutdown or rollback, evidence of system modification after governance intervention, board reporting cadence | Policy-only oversight, no tested control, no documented intervention, no verified override capability |

## 6. Issuance Standard

Not every analytical output should be presented as a public letter grade.

| Output type | What Alpha can show | Rubric standard |
|-------------|---------------------|-----------------|
| **Public Profile** | Full analytical content without a public letter grade | Public evidence only. Alpha can analyze posture, flags, and dimension patterns, but does not publicly issue the full AAA-D scale. |
| **Assessed Rating** | Full letter grade, trajectory, and watch status | Direct engagement and founder review by Steven Wolfe Pereira allow Alpha to apply the complete grade architecture, including assessed-only D12 treatment. |
| **Alpha Estimate** | Clearly labeled directional view | Never presented as equivalent to an assessed rating. Useful for internal triage, not for public-grade claims. |

## 8. Hard Constraints

Alpha uses explicit floor and ceiling rules so that a company cannot bury a critical governance failure inside a strong average.

### Materiality curve

Companies with the highest AI materiality face a stricter composite curve:

- **Critical materiality:** composite multiplied by `0.92`
- **High materiality:** composite multiplied by `0.96`
- **Moderate or Low materiality:** no downward adjustment

### General floor rules

These apply across D1-D10:

| Trigger | Maximum grade |
|---------|---------------|
| Any applicable dimension below 30/100 | **D** |
| Any applicable dimension at 30-39/100 | **CC** |
| Any applicable dimension at 40-49/100 | **B** |

### Universal floor rules

| Trigger | Maximum grade |
|---------|---------------|
| **D1 Leadership & Accountability** below 50/100 | **A** |
| **D8 Regulatory & Compliance Posture** below 40/100 | **BBB** |

### Profile-specific floors

| Profile | Trigger | Maximum grade |
|---------|---------|---------------|
| **Developer** | D2 below 50/100 | **BBB** |
| **Developer** | D9 below 50/100 | **A** |
| **Deployer** | D6 below 50/100 | **BBB** |
| **Deployer** | D10 below 50/100 | **A** |
| **Hybrid** | D2 below 50/100 | **BBB** |
| **Hybrid** | D6, D9, or D10 below 50/100 | **A** |

### Corrigibility floor rules

These are the most important assessed-rating constraints in the framework:

| D12 condition | Maximum grade | Why it exists |
|---------------|---------------|---------------|
| **D12 = 0** | **B** | No evidence governance has ever stopped, modified, or constrained a deployed AI system |
| **D12 below 20/100** | **BB** | Override architecture may exist on paper, but it has not been demonstrated or independently verified |

Public Profiles do not use the assessed-rating D12 ceiling in the same way, because public evidence is structurally weaker and public letter grades are not issued.

### Concern ceilings

The concern screen operates independently from dimension scoring:

| Concern result | Maximum grade |
|----------------|---------------|
| Any **Disqualifying** concern | **D** |
| Any **Severe** concern | **CCC** |
| Two or more **Elevated** concerns | **BB** |
| One **Elevated** concern | **A** |

## 9. Current Review Standard

Before Alpha issues or changes an Assessed Rating, Steven Wolfe Pereira, as Alpha's current methodology owner and reviewer, should be able to answer "yes" to all five questions below:

1. Does the evidence support the score, or only the narrative?
2. Are the highest-weight indicators backed by hard-to-game proof?
3. Has the company shown governance in practice, not just governance on paper?
4. Does any floor, concern ceiling, or corrigibility ceiling apply?
5. Would a skeptical board committee accept this rationale without needing unstated assumptions?

If the answer to any question is no, the rating should not move up.
